Material test reference

Target visuals and numbers for tuning bullet-vs-material effects, redrawn from the reference photos and plots you supplied. The animated side views are procedural and deterministic, so they can be scrubbed to the same timestamps as the photos and compared frame by frame. Numbers marked measured come from the lab figures; the comparison infographics are illustrative only (see the last section).

2. Concrete panel perforation, three strengths (ms scale)

Reference: front and back high-speed views at about 155 m/s. Here one cross-section shows both sides: front spall cone, back scab cone, crack fan between. Bullet scale 66 px long, 1.6 px per mm, panel 45 mm thick (assumed, not stated in the figures).

Contact sheet, one column per strength, rows at the reference timestamps

What to match

2–5 msScab cone detaches: dust plus angular chips on the back. C35 gives many small pieces and heavy dust, C75 fewer and bigger, C110 two or three large slabs.
6–12 msBullet stub (dark, flattened) travels on at the residual speed inside or behind the debris cloud. Front cloud keeps billowing, longest for C110.

3. Damage footprints and cross-sections (measured)

Front spall and back scab per panel, from the photo with dimensions. Procedural stand-ins: same width and height, ragged outline, central bullet hole.

Rule from the sections: the damage is an hourglass. A small front spall cone (about 80–100 mm wide), a narrow waist where the bullet tunnelled, and a much larger back scab cone (150–220 mm). The back scab is 1.9× (C35), 1.8× (C75) and 2.75× (C110) the front spall width, so stronger concrete scabs relatively wider.

4. Velocity loss and ballistic limit (measured)

Projectile speed vs time, one slab per strength at vi near 155 m/s. Most of the loss is within the first 0.5 ms, then a slow tail.

5. The comparison infographics

The poster-style images (cartridge by material grids, wound-size posters, "sniper impact" panels) look AI-generated and some numbers in them are not plausible (for example 57 mm exit wounds, impact energy labels that disagree with each other). Use them only for the qualitative look per material, not as test targets.

MaterialLook that is consistent across all the posters
Pine woodDark core hole, splinter fan and radial grain cracks. Size grows with calibre. Buckshot gives separate small holes.
DrywallClean hole in the front, paper torn outwards into ragged petals on the back, white dust. Big rounds blow out a large irregular hole.
Car door steelSmall rounds: dent or shallow pit. Rifle rounds: round perforation with a bent-in, petalled rim. Buckshot: several small dents or holes.
GlassCrater at the centre with radial cracks plus concentric rings. Laminated glass stays in one piece. Larger rounds cause a bigger white spall zone.
AR500 steelDishing, bright polished dent with lead splash, no perforation for pistol rounds. Rifle rounds leave a deeper crater with a raised rim.
Ballistic gelPermanent cavity along the axis, wider for expanding or tumbling rounds. Already covered by docs/gel-154.
ConcreteSpalling: pale crater with radial cracks on the front, a larger scab on the back (matches section 3).
